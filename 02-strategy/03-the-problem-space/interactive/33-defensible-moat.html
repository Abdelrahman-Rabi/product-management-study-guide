Your goal

Acknowledge feature copying decay kinetics, contrast traditional and platform-based defensive strategies, and map tech companies to their correct moat configurations.

Lecture overview

To survive in the long run, your product must build defensible barriers to block competitors. In the technology industry, successful features are copied rapidly, causing them to decay from customer delighters to baseline expectations.

1. Feature Copying and Kano Decay

Successful features get copied. This copying drives Kano Feature Decay. A new capability starts as a Delighter, decays into a Performance Feature as other competitors build it, and finally decays into a Must-Have (table stakes) that customers take for granted. Uber's live driver tracking map is a classic example of this decay cycle.

2. Five Traditional Defensible Moats

Functional features are copied, so companies build structural barriers. The 5 traditional moats are: Economics of Scale, Network Effects (FB friend density), IP/Patents, High Switching Costs (complex setups that can trigger customer resentment if they feel trapped), and Customer Loyalty (the best traditional barrier).

3. Modern Technology Moats

Modern tech leverages Platform and Community moats. Platforms (Salesforce, AWS, Twilio) allow developers to build their applications on top of your technology base, generating customer dependency. Communities link customers to each other, creating belonging and collective value that competitors cannot duplicate.

Strategic Summary: Product Growth Loop

The complete Section 3 roadmap for product scaling is: Start with a Big Problem -> Design a Timeless Solution -> Pick a Growing Market -> Align your Brand Promise -> Build a Defensible Moat.

Common Misunderstandings

"We will stay ahead of competitors by constantly building new features"

Correction: Features are copied fast. Constant feature development without a structural moat (like network effects or platform integrations) turns into a feature factory treadmills. Defensibility comes from structural ecosystems, not functional feature checklists.

"Contractual locks are a good way to secure high switching costs"

Correction: Forcing enterprise customers to stay via contracts or integration complexity leads to customer resentment. The best switching cost is value-based (e.g. the product holds so much custom configuration value that transferring is operationally illogical for the client, but they still love the tool).

Key concepts and definitions

  • Kano Feature Decay: The decay of product capabilities from delighters to must-haves over time as competitors copy them.
  • Network Effect: A phenomenon where a product becomes more valuable to each user as the total user base grows.
  • Platform Moat: A defensible barrier built by allowing third-party developers to construct applications on your core infrastructure.
  • Customer Resentment: The loss of brand equity that occurs when customers are locked in by complex workarounds rather than product satisfaction.

Moat Matching Challenge

Match each tech corporation to its principal defensible moat.

Facebook

Incorrect. Facebook's pricing is free for users; its moat is driven by user relationships.
Correct! People stay on Facebook because their network of friends is established there, representing a classic Network Effect moat.
Incorrect. Facebook is an interaction network, not an application infrastructure base for third-party tools.

Salesforce.com

Correct! Salesforce allows developers to build entire applications on top of its databases, locking in enterprise operations and systems integration.
Incorrect. Salesforce has communities, but its primary, highest-barrier moat is the Salesforce platform database integrations.
Incorrect. Patent law does not defend the core of Salesforce's CRM market dominance; its ecosystem dependency does.
